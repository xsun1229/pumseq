Dispersion (φ) describes variation in pU rates beyond binomial read-sampling noise, after accounting for the covariates. We tested how well it can be estimated at individual sites using simulations based on our data.

Small φ is difficult to estimate at many sites. Estimates often approach zero, especially when the data contain little information. Shrinkage reduces this pile-up, but at small φ it often returns a value near the shared prior centre rather than distinguishing individual sites.

1. Can the MLE recover a known φ?

For each site, we keep the real read depths and the expected pU rates fitted from the real data using three phenotype PCs. We choose a known φ, simulate counts from the beta-binomial model, and refit the model to those counts.

Each panel below uses one true φ for all sites. We repeat this at six values from 0.005 to 0.4, with about 10,000 sites and typically 49 covered cell lines per site. The generating means vary across sites and cell lines; the refit estimates the means and φ from the simulated counts.

2. Read depth and mean pU both matter

The simulations cover six true φ values, from 0.005 to 0.4. The heatmaps show how recovery changes with both dispersion and read depth. Depth groups are fixed across the six settings, using quintiles of median depth among covered cell lines in the real-data templates.

Darker cells mean larger percentages in every panel: higher is better in the accuracy panel, but worse in the underestimation and failure panels. Read the failure panel alongside accuracy, since excluding failed fits can change which sites contribute to the comparison. More than tenfold underestimation is a relative error criterion; it is not the same as reaching the numerical floor.

Low mean pU is also associated with estimates near zero. To examine it alongside depth, we group sites by their real-data pooled pU percentage (converted reads divided by total reads). This is a summary of the observed rate, not the full vector of fitted cell-line-specific means used to generate the simulations.

The separation between the two pU groups can be compared across depth and true φ. These comparisons do not isolate causal effects: sites differ in their depth distributions and fitted means. A controlled simulation varying the mean while holding depth and φ fixed would separate those effects more directly. These results do not yet establish a universal depth cutoff for reporting per-site φ.

3. Does shrinkage improve estimation?

We compared ordinary maximum likelihood (MLE) with several ways to stabilise φ. MAP estimation maximises the likelihood plus a log prior, pulling weakly supported estimates toward a shared distribution.

Approach Purpose Main observation
Multiple optimiser starts Find a better likelihood optimum Helps with local optima; does not remove the small-φ pile-up.
Select an optimum near the population mode Move estimates away from the boundary Does little to improve per-site tracking.
Predict a prior centre from site properties Allow the centre to vary by site The explored predictors explained little variation in estimated φ.
Fixed-centre Gaussian or Student-t prior Shrink extreme estimates Reduces boundary estimates, but can pull genuinely large φ downward.
Two-component prior Allow low- and high-dispersion groups Tracking at large φ is similar to MLE.
Moment-derived prior Learn the prior from moment estimates Gives the strongest tracking at larger φ among the approaches tested; tracking remains weak at small φ.

For this comparison, true φ varies between sites. We draw site-specific values from log-normal distributions centred at 0.0126, 0.05, 0.2, or 0.4, restricted to [10⁻⁵, 0.9]. Counts again use the real depths and fitted means.

The moment-derived approach estimates the mean anew from each simulated dataset, calculates a moment estimate of φ, and uses the collection of estimates to build a Student-t prior on log₁₀φ. We compare three rules for the prior centre:

  • mom-median: median of all finite moment estimates, including negatives.
  • mom-mode: density mode on the log scale among positive estimates.
  • mom-q60: 60th percentile of all finite moment estimates.

The prior width uses the spread of positive log-moment estimates. The prior is rebuilt for each simulation setting before the MAP refits. The moment calculation is closed form, but it uses means from a fitted model.

How we compare methods

Being within a factor of two of the truth is useful, but insufficient: if true values are tightly clustered, a constant guess can score well. We also report:

  • Spearman correlation: whether estimates rank sites in the right order.
  • Log–log slope: how strongly estimates change with truth; a slope near zero indicates little response, while a slope near one indicates similar scaling.

Neither measure alone establishes accurate estimates. The constant benchmark uses the true median, which is available only in simulation; its correlation is undefined.

At the smallest centre, shrinkage methods have correlations of about 0.40–0.42: they improve on MLE, but still distinguish sites poorly. The moment-derived mom-mode and mom-q60 rules perform better at larger φ, reaching correlations around 0.77–0.78 in the setting centred at 0.4. Differences between nearby scores should not be overinterpreted without repeated simulations.
